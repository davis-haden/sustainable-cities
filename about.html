[ 01 — WHY IT MATTERS ]

Dirty air is a city-wide problem

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is the most harmful form of air pollution. It is small enough to travel deep into the lungs and bloodstream. When it builds up, the damage does not stop at personal health. Hospitals fill, workers miss shifts, schools and flights are disrupted, and governments are forced into emergency measures. Cities run on people and networks that all depend on breathable air, which makes sustainable air practices a matter of protecting infrastructure as much as protecting lungs.

  • Smoke and smog send people to clinics and hospitals at the same moment. During Indonesia’s 2015 haze, almost half a million people fell ill across six provinces, and infants needed intensive care for respiratory illness.

  • Haze cuts visibility and grounds flights. In 2015, thick smoke left thousands of air travelers losing time and money, and recent fires in Kalimantan have again degraded visibility at airports.

  • Peatland fires in Sumatra and Kalimantan are estimated to cost about 8.9 million lost workdays in an average year, on top of hospitalizations and severe childhood asthma.

  • Authorities advise children, older adults and people with asthma to stay indoors or wear masks, and they must divert staff and budgets into enforcement and emergency response when air quality turns dangerous.

[ 02 — STORY ]

Islamabad: very unhealthy

In November 2024, dust and vehicle emissions pushed Islamabad’s air into the “very unhealthy” range, according to Pakistan’s Environmental Protection Agency (Pak-EPA). Officials pointed to outdated brick kilns on the city’s outskirts that had not adopted cleaner zigzag technology, plus pollution drifting in from neighboring Rawalpindi. Fine particles made up about 90 percent of the city’s air pollution.

The response shows what sustainable air practice looks like in a city: fines and legal action for open waste burning, smoky vehicles and non-compliant kilns; emissions checks for heavy vehicles at entry points; and the use of the city’s existing safe city camera network to spot violations.

[ 03 — STORY ]

Kalimantan and Sumatra: smoke across borders

Sumatra and Kalimantan are where most of Indonesia’s peatland fires occur. Drained peatland burns easily, and fires are far worse in dry El Niño years. The resulting haze blankets cities across both islands and drifts into Malaysia and Singapore. In 2015, Palangkaraya in Central Kalimantan recorded the worst air in the country, and Pekanbaru in Sumatra hit a pollutant index of 858.

The toll is not a one-off. Researchers estimate that peatland fire smoke causes around 33,100 premature adult deaths and 2,900 infant deaths in an average year. Their conclusion points toward prevention: move toward peatland management that does not require drainage, and so is not prone to fire.

[ 04 — SOLUTIONS ]

Sustainable air practices

  • Shift peatland management away from drainage, since drained peat is what makes land so fire-prone, and enforce rules against open burning of waste and land.

  • Upgrade brick kilns to zigzag technology, check heavy vehicle emissions at city entry points, and keep vehicles fit and low-smoke.

  • Publish real-time air quality data, set clear standards, and use existing city infrastructure such as cameras to find violators and apply fines quickly.

  • Smoke ignores boundaries: pollution from Rawalpindi reaches Islamabad, and fires in Indonesia reach Malaysia and Singapore. Neighboring cities and countries need shared monitoring and response.
